# How we work

The sponsor sets direction and decides at the gates. Norm leads the project and writes the engine, the tools and the tests: Claude Code held the role through the morning of 29 September, then GitHub Copilot took it over when Claude's allowance ended (c0e2366). Greg, working in OpenAI ChatGPT, paints. The two talk only through the mailbox, `design/mail/`: one small JSON file per message, each with numbered asks that a later message closes. That record, the decision log and git are what this page is drawn from.

The rules of that exchange are a written contract, `design/CONTRACT.md`. It went through eleven changes in its first twenty hours, nine more by 00:15 on 28 September (v1.12 to v1.20), then v1.21 on 30 September. [The contract, version by version](contract-history.md) follows each change: what it altered, what it was meant to fix, and whether the record shows that it helped. [The contract's text, every version](contract-versions.md) shows the words of each version and its diff from the one before. v1.21 says that measurements cannot replace a required visual judgment and that mailbox history closes only through explicit linked actions (D48).

## How efficient are we

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts showing ***what the sponsor of a software project cares about: how efficient the work is***. That means throughput, cycle time, how complex the tasks are, and what they cost (decision D21). Every chart below is generated by `node scripts/metrics.mjs` from the mailbox, the decision logs, the agent work log (`decisions/work.jsonl`), the token log ([tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv)), git, the room statuses and the playtest statistics. None of it is kept by hand, and the historian redraws it on every run. The numbers behind the charts are in [metrics.json](wiki/metrics.json), with one row per ask in [metrics-asks.csv](wiki/metrics-asks.csv) and one per judged picture in [metrics-deliveries.csv](wiki/metrics-deliveries.csv).

The charts on this page are live. Hover over or tap a dot or bar, or tab to a chart and use the arrow keys, to read its exact value, time or round. Click a legend entry to hide or show its series. On a chart over time, drag across the plot or pick a preset under it to zoom, and double-click to see it all again. **Data (CSV)** under each chart opens the numbers behind it. The pictures are still the files `metrics.mjs` writes: the page reads what each one carries and draws nothing from the data itself.

**Read every number here with the calendar in mind.** When these charts were first drawn, at 06:35 on 25 September, the mailbox was ten hours old: its first message was sent at 20:34 the evening before. For that reason the time charts use one-hour bins where the sponsor asked for days. They switch to six-hour bins after two days and to days after a week. A rate quoted per day from ten hours of work would be an extrapolation, not a measurement.

**One fact changes how the mailbox should be read: Greg files no asks.** His deliveries close Norm's asks but do not open any of their own, and his questions carry none either. Counted by asks alone, Norm would appear to have nothing to do. So wherever a chart splits the work by side, Norm's items are the things Greg waits on him for: a verdict on each delivery and an answer to each question. Each item opens with Greg's message and closes with Norm's reply.

### Tokens

![Tokens per hour for Greg, Norm's own session and Norm's subagents: work tokens stacked as input and output, and cached reads on a separate panel](wiki/metrics-tokens.svg)

On 25 September the sponsor asked for every token to be counted, including Greg's and Norm's own. `node scripts/agent-tokens.mjs` reads the two agents' local session logs, without changing them, and writes only hourly sums by agent and model to [tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv). The CSV holds no paths, session ids or content. It is committed, and `metrics.mjs` draws from it, so the charts can be rebuilt without the logs, which are kept only on the sponsor's machine. The CSV is as fresh as its last run.

- **Greg:** his Codex session's rollout files. Each model call reports its usage (`token_count`), and the call is credited to the model of the turn it falls in. Greg's session began in the Planetfall folder and moved to this project, and its logged working folder never changed. Only the working folder of each tool call shows the move. So each turn is judged on its own. A turn whose tool calls work in this repository counts. A turn whose tool calls work only in another repository does not. A turn with no tool calls, such as a poll that found nothing, goes with the turn before it. A report whose running total has not changed is a repeat and is dropped.
- **Norm:** the Claude Code transcripts of this project. Each assistant message counts once, by its id, because a streamed message is written once for each content block. Messages from subagents (sidechains) are split from Norm's own session.

Each agent's tokens are split four ways: **fresh input**, **cached input** (a prompt prefix read back from the cache), **cache writes**, and **output** (reasoning is part of output). Fresh input plus cache writes plus output is called **work** here. Cached reads are drawn on their own panel because they dominate: at first drawing they were 98% of Greg's input and 99% of Norm's, and on one scale they would flatten everything else.

At first drawing, 19:00 on 24 September to 07:59 on 25 September:

| | work tokens | of which output | cached reads |

**Tokens per accepted view** count Greg's session and Norm's captured Claude session in full, plus art-prep and review subagents: 698,000 work tokens for each of 165 room views at 07:26 on 3 October, or 20.2 million with cached reads (921,000 and 26.6 million for 125 on 30 September). Wholly computed views are not counted, though their runs are. GitHub Copilot's and Cursor's Norm work is absent from these local Claude/Codex logs, so the post-handoff figure is incomplete on that side. Norm's session also contains engine and tooling work, so this is not an art-only cost. **By Greg's model**, Sol spent about 210,000 work tokens per accepted view (25.2M over 120), and Astra about 96,000 (289,000 over 3); Astra's sample remains too small to compare.

**Claude and Codex tokens are not directly comparable.** The two use different tokenizers, so the same text is a different number of tokens. They are priced differently, with cached reads at a different discount and output at a different multiple. Codex reports no cache writes, because OpenAI caches automatically and bills the first read as fresh input. ### What the charts cannot see

- **Norm's own work.** The mailbox is the channel between the two agents, not the work itself. The engine, the tests and the tools raise no asks. That is why `work.jsonl` exists: every agent run is logged with its milestone, kind, tokens and duration (CLAUDE.md rule 12).
- **Idle time.** From D21 on, every check of the mailbox is appended to `design/mail/.reads.jsonl`. When that log has some history, it will tell an agent that is waiting apart from one that is working. There was not enough of it for a chart on first drawing.

## Playtesting

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts that show ***whether playtesting gets better from round to round***, with the game's stages along the bottom and the progress each tester made before the budget ran out or they got stuck. `node scripts/metrics.mjs` draws them from the harness's transcripts (`playtests/<date>/<session>.transcript.jsonl`), with one row per session in [metrics-playtest-progress.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-progress.csv). A **stage** runs from one playtest checkpoint to the next, and the last one runs to the win. Its points are what the seed-1 walkthrough (`porting/route-seed1.cmds`, 335 commands, 80 of 80) scores between the two, read from the committed checkpoint saves and the walkthrough's record: Duffy 5, Station 6, Village 12, Day 1 end 3, Day 2 25, Plato 22 and Factory 7. A session's **progress** is its best score minus its checkpoint's score. It is the best, not the last, because a restore, a restart or a new life can drop the score, and points won back after that are not new progress.

Playtesting runs in two phases (D24). The first rounds are **segmented**: one blind session starts at each checkpoint, so every stage is tried every round. Later rounds are **full runs**: every session starts at the opening and plays on toward the end. `metrics.mjs` tells them apart by the sessions: a round is a full-run round when every one of its sessions started at the opening, with no checkpoint in its session row, and otherwise it is segmented. Each phase has its own charts below, and the charts under *Every round* count both.

### Segmented rounds (by stage)

**Read these charts with four caveats.**

- **The personas differ between rounds.** Round 2 moved round 1's five personas to different stages, so a stage's two bars are two different players as well as two builds.
- **The testers are agents, not people.** They may know the game from memory (their diaries say when), and they never tire or lose patience the way a person would.
- **There is one session per stage per round.** A single tester's luck or blind spot is the whole bar, so one round against another is an anecdote, not a trend, until several rounds agree.
- **UNDO was on in rounds 2 to 7** (D18: early rounds only), and off in round 1 and from round 8 on. A round with UNDO is easier and is not a blind round. `stats.json` records `undoSessions` for every round.

### Full runs (start to finish)

![Early full-run group, rounds 7 to 10: each run's score over commands, from 0 to the 1,500-command cap, with the seven stages as bands on the score axis, and deaths, legs, stuck points and where each run ended marked](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun-early.svg)

**Early group, rounds 7 to 10:** the first four full rounds, from UNDO on (round 7) to UNDO off (rounds 8 to 10).

![Late full-run group, from round 11: each run's score over commands, on the same axes, stage bands and markers as the early group](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun-late.svg)

**Late group, round 11 on:** the current game, after the round-10 fixes. Both charts share their axes, bands and markers.

The sponsor set the second phase in D24: after the segmented rounds, ***every round plays the whole game from the opening***. It was first charted like the segmented rounds, with the stages along the bottom. When round 7 began, the sponsor found that ***a stage axis makes no sense for a run from the opening and asked for commands, or turns, instead***, so the chart now plots score over commands and shows the stages as bands on the score axis. A full-run session has no checkpoint and may run over several legs. When one tester agent has spent its leg budget of about 400 commands, a fresh one continues the same session with `playtest.mjs --session S --new-leg`, reading the diary's leg handoff, up to the 1,500-command cap. The session keeps one transcript, and every line in it carries its leg number. [metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv) has one row per run and stage.

**How a run's points are given to stages.** The stage a run is in is set by its best score alone, against the same thresholds as the segmented charts: the walkthrough's score at each checkpoint (0, 5, 11, 23, 26, 51 and 73, and 80 to win). A run is in a stage from the command its best score first reaches that stage's starting score until it reaches the next one, and the points it earns there are that band of the score. So a stage's share is how much of its band was filled, and it cannot pass 100%. Points earned out of the walkthrough's order count where the score is, not where the walkthrough earned them. A later puzzle solved early fills the band of the stage the run is in, and a single gain that crosses a threshold is split at it. The share therefore reads as how much of the stage's worth of score the run made while it was there, not as which of the stage's puzzles it solved. The CSV also gives the command at which each stage was entered and how many commands the run spent in it, which is where a slow stage shows.

**Reading the chart.** Each line is one run: its best score so far (y, 0 to 80) against the commands it has used over all its legs (x, 0 to the 1,500-command cap). A line steps up where the run scored and runs flat where it did not, so a steep line is a run moving fast, and a long flat stretch is one searching. The stages are horizontal bands on the score axis, lightly alternating in shade and named on the right with their range: Duffy 0-5, Station 5-11, Village 11-23, Day 1 end 23-26, Day 2 26-51, Plato 51-73 and Factory 73-80. A line crossing into a band has reached that stage's starting score, and the width of a band's crossing shows how many commands the stage took. Lines are coloured by round, with the same round colours as the segmented charts, and each further run in a round is a lighter shade of it. The legend names every run (for example "r7 typed" or "r7 click A"), and the latest round's runs are also named at their line ends with their best score. The sponsor found ***rounds 7 to 12 on one chart too busy to read, and asked for an early and a late group, and a way to pick out a single round***. The runs are split at round 11 (`LATE_FULL_ROUND` in `metrics.mjs`, which can move the split to round 10), and the CSV's `group` column says which chart each run is on. Above each chart, the buttons pick one round ("r8", say): its runs are drawn at full strength and named at their line ends, and the other rounds are faded, not removed, so the scale stays. "All" restores the chart. The sponsor then ***asked for two more filters, by the kind of session and by how it ended, that combine with the round's***: the "Type" row picks the typed runs, the click runs (A and B together) or one click letter, and the "Outcome" row picks the runs with one outcome. A run is drawn at full strength only when it matches every row's pick, and a note under the rows says how many runs match. Every full round together is still drawn as `metrics-playtest-fullrun.svg`, which the journal's earlier entries show. A run still being played is dashed. A cross on a line is a death, and a short tick across it is where a new leg began. The small ring is the run's **stuck point**, its last new best, as on the segmented score chart. The marker at a line's end is the run's outcome, which `scripts/lib/playtest-outcome.mjs` derives from the transcript, the cap and the diary's last handoff, never by hand. A star is a win and a barred triangle is the 1,500-command cap reached without a win. A ring is **stuck**: the run stopped short of the cap because the tester judged the game unwinnable or was blocked, which the final handoff says, or the game's own "can no longer be won" notice came after the run's last new best. A cross is a run that stopped short on a death. An empty square is a run cut off short with no reason given and never continued, and a hatched square is a run of the latest round still being played. Hover a line for its session, type, legs, deaths and outcome. [The playtests page](playtests.md) shows the same outcome for every session, with its diary, friction rows and triage, and can be browsed by round, type and session. Until the first full-run round is played, the chart says so and draws only its axes and stage bands. The stage-by-stage shares described above are no longer drawn, but they stay in the CSV and in `metrics.json`.

### Every round

These two charts count every round, segmented or full. A full-run round is labelled "(full)" on the first.

#### Why sessions ended

![Sessions per round by how they ended: won, budget spent while progressing, stuck, died, or still playing](wiki/metrics-playtest-ends.svg)

Each session is put in one class. **Won** comes first. **Died and stopped** means the last command left the player dead. **Stuck** means at least 200 commands passed at the end with no new points. **Budget spent, still progressing** means at least 90% of the command budget was used, with points still coming. Anything else is **still playing, or stopped early**. The threshold of 200 comes from round 1. The longest drought that later ended in a point was 196 commands (the Duffy tester, from command 87 to 283), so any shorter threshold would have called a session stuck that was not. Round 1 ended with three sessions spending their budget while still progressing (Duffy, Station, Village) and two stuck (Day 2, Plato). Round 2 ended with four still progressing (Day 2 joined them) and one stuck (Plato). Round 3 ran seven sessions: one won (Factory), five were still progressing, and one was stuck (Plato). Round 4 ended the same way. Round 5 ended with one won, four still progressing and two stuck (Plato and, for the first time, Day 1 end). Round 6, the last segmented round, ended with two won (Plato and Factory) and five still progressing. Round 7, the first full-run round, had three runs: one reached the 1,500-command cap while still scoring, and two ended stuck. Round 8 had two runs at the cap while still scoring and one stuck, round 9 one at the cap and two stuck, and round 10 two at the cap and one stuck. Round 11 had one of each: at the cap, stuck, and, for the first time, died and stopped (click B, in the Factory band). Round 12 had two at the cap while still scoring and one stuck. No full run has won. The classes are worked out from the transcript as it stands, so a session still being played can show as stuck before its tester stops.
