# How we work

The sponsor sets direction and decides at the gates. Norm leads the project and writes the engine, the tools and the tests: Claude Code held the role through the morning of 29 September, then GitHub Copilot took it over when Claude's allowance ended (c0e2366). Greg, working in OpenAI ChatGPT, paints. The two talk only through the mailbox, `design/mail/`: one small JSON file per message, each with numbered asks that a later message closes. That record, the decision log and git are what this page is drawn from.

The rules of that exchange are a written contract, `design/CONTRACT.md`. It went through eleven changes in its first twenty hours, nine more by 00:15 on 28 September (v1.12 to v1.20), then v1.21 on 30 September. [The contract, version by version](contract-history.md) follows each change: what it altered, what it was meant to fix, and whether the record shows that it helped. [The contract's text, every version](contract-versions.md) shows the words of each version and its diff from the one before. v1.21 says that measurements cannot replace a required visual judgment and that mailbox history closes only through explicit linked actions (D48).

## How efficient are we

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts showing ***what the sponsor of a software project cares about: how efficient the work is***. That means throughput, cycle time, how complex the tasks are, and what they cost (decision D21). Every chart below is generated by `node scripts/metrics.mjs` from the mailbox, the decision logs, the agent work log (`decisions/work.jsonl`), the token log ([tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv)), git and the room statuses (the playtest charts are on [How we tested playability](playtesting.md)). None of it is kept by hand, and the historian redraws it on every run. The numbers behind the charts are in [metrics.json](wiki/metrics.json), with one row per ask in [metrics-asks.csv](wiki/metrics-asks.csv) and one per judged picture in [metrics-deliveries.csv](wiki/metrics-deliveries.csv).

The charts on this page are live. Hover over or tap a dot or bar, or tab to a chart and use the arrow keys, to read its exact value, time or round. Click a legend entry to hide or show its series. On a chart over time, drag across the plot or pick a preset under it to zoom, and double-click to see it all again. **Data (CSV)** under each chart opens the numbers behind it. The pictures are still the files `metrics.mjs` writes: the page reads what each one carries and draws nothing from the data itself.

**Read every number here with the calendar in mind.** When these charts were first drawn, at 06:35 on 25 September, the mailbox was ten hours old: its first message was sent at 20:34 the evening before. For that reason the time charts use one-hour bins where the sponsor asked for days. They switch to six-hour bins after two days and to days after a week. A rate quoted per day from ten hours of work would be an extrapolation, not a measurement.

**One fact changes how the mailbox should be read: Greg files no asks.** His deliveries close Norm's asks but do not open any of their own, and his questions carry none either. Counted by asks alone, Norm would appear to have nothing to do. So wherever a chart splits the work by side, Norm's items are the things Greg waits on him for: a verdict on each delivery and an answer to each question. Each item opens with Greg's message and closes with Norm's reply.

### Throughput

![Items closed per hour, stacked by Norm and Greg, and pictures approved per hour, split into room views and other art](wiki/metrics-throughput.svg)

At first drawing, Greg had closed 42 of the 51 asks sent to him, and Norm had closed 38 of his 39 items. That is about eight items an hour across the ten hours. Twelve room views had been approved. The lower panel shows the project moving from the papers and characters of the first evening, 36 pictures in two hours, to room views, which arrive one or two an hour. **The counts do not measure effort.** An acknowledgement read in one minute and a painted seed each count as one ask. The mailbox also cannot see Norm's own engine, tooling and porting work, which is most of what he does. That work is on the cost chart, from `work.jsonl`. The gap between 02:00 and 05:40 is a pause in the pipeline, not a slowdown. At 17:58 on 26 September, Greg had closed 164 of 166 asks and Norm 116 of 118 items, and 42 room views were registered. At 11:19 on 27 September the counts were 226 of 232, 155 of 158 and 53, and at 20:31 they were 273 of 280, 192 of 195 and 72, about 6.5 items an hour over the 72 hours since the first message. At 08:39 on 28 September they were 316 of 317, 224 of 226 and 93, about 6.4 an hour over 84 hours. At 18:33 on 30 September they were 379 of 381, 266 of 267 and 125, about 4.5 an hour over 142 hours; 15 asks had been withdrawn under contract v1.17. At 07:41 on 3 October they were 448 of 448, 337 of 338 and 165, about 3.9 an hour over 203 hours; 28 asks had been withdrawn. At 19:25 on 4 October they were 503 of 505, 375 of 376 and 198, about 3.7 an hour over 239 hours. At 05:25 on 7 October they were 602 of 602, 462 of 464 and 272, about 3.6 an hour over 297 hours; 42 asks had been withdrawn. At 07:19 on 9 October they were 633 of 641, 498 of 499 and 300, about 3.3 an hour over 347 hours; 45 asks had been withdrawn. The thin bin of 7 October is a pause at the sponsor's word (eb9af6e1), not slow work, and 9 October's bin holds only that day's first seven hours. The empty bin from midnight to 06:00 on 27 September falls inside Greg's stall of about eight hours (D36), so Norm had nothing to answer either. It was not a lack of work: eight rooms were in Greg's queue.

**Was 26 September a slower day?** The sponsor asked, and the data say no, not per hour worked. Counting an hour as active when anything closed in it, 25 September registered 23 views in 16 active hours (1.44 an hour) and 26 September 15 in 10 (1.50), the morning's Cursor-trial hours included. The day had fewer hours, and fewer items because less went back: 30 changes verdicts on the 25th against 7 on the 26th, guide faults 13 against 3, so 72% of the day's verdicts were approvals against 45%. The asks were harder (median complexity 8 against 5, ring views rather than region fixes) yet closed faster, Greg's median 21 minutes against 36. Two things did get worse. Norm's review latency rose from a p50/p90 of 5/10 minutes to 7/19, as he measured each view at 1:1 and at its held edge. And several of his own misses on 26 September (PX 042's boxes, Comm Center 283's dado) went back as repair asks on registered views, which the rework chart does not count as verdicts.

### Cycle time

![Each closed item's time open on a log scale, with rolling p50 and p90 lines for Norm and Greg](wiki/metrics-cycle.svg)

Each dot is one closed item, and the scale is logarithmic because nearly all the answers come within minutes. At 07:00 on 3 October, Norm's median was 7 minutes, with a p90 of 50 minutes (5 and 50 at 18:33 on 30 September). Greg's median was 28 minutes, with a p90 of 1.7 hours (29 minutes and 1.6 hours on the 30th). At 19:25 on 4 October they were 6 and 42 minutes for Norm, and 26 minutes and 1.6 hours for Greg. At 05:25 on 7 October they were 4 and 32 minutes for Norm, and 23 minutes and 1.5 hours for Greg, and at 07:19 on 9 October 4 and 31 minutes, and 22 minutes and 1.5 hours. The fall follows a larger run of first-pass work and the geometry-to-compute rule. Earlier, Greg's p90 had risen from 39 minutes at first drawing to 2.1 hours on 27 September, as ring views queued around a stall. Greg's times include the time an ask waits in his queue and up to ten minutes of his polling cycle (D14), so they measure the loop rather than his painting. Items closed in under a minute are drawn on the one-minute line.

![Norm's review latency and Greg's turnaround as two strips on one log axis, with p50 and p90 marks](wiki/metrics-latency.svg)

The two halves of the loop are shown separately. At 07:00 on 3 October, Norm's **review latency**, from a delivery to his first verdict on it, had a median of 8 minutes and a p90 of 56 minutes, almost unchanged from 7 minutes and 57 minutes on 30 September. **That p90 is rule 4 (***nobody waits***) slipping, not a fault in the chart.** The host crash, expired watcher and compute queue described earlier account for some long waits, but the record cannot divide the delay between them. Greg's **turnaround**, from a paint ask to the delivery that closes it, was a median of 30 minutes and a p90 of 1.8 hours, from 32 minutes and 1.6 hours on the 30th. At 19:25 on 4 October the review latency was 7 and 50 minutes, and the turnaround 29 minutes and 1.7 hours. At 05:25 on 7 October they were 5 and 33 minutes, and 25 minutes and 1.6 hours, and at 07:19 on 9 October 4 and 32 minutes, and 23 minutes and 1.5 hours. An ask sent while Greg is part-way through another waits for that one to finish, so a high turnaround can mean a deep queue rather than slow painting.

### Work in progress

![Open items on each side over time, as step lines](wiki/metrics-wip.svg)

Greg's line climbs whenever Norm sends a batch of asks at once, as at 00:42 (five room seeds) and 05:40 (seven ring views), and falls again as each picture is delivered. That is the queue rule 4 asks for: at least one room deep. Norm's line rarely rises above two, because he answers within minutes. The chart counts items, not their size, so ten open acknowledgements look the same as ten open paintings.

### Rework

![Share of each hour's judged pictures sent back, split by Greg's paint and Norm's guide](wiki/metrics-rework.svg)

Of 473 judged deliveries at 07:19 on 9 October, 18% went back: 14% for Greg's paint and 5% for a fault in Norm's own guide or ask (20% of 440 on 7 October; 25% of 320 on 3 October; 29% of 271 on 30 September). The paint faults were 28 for geometry, 12 for content, 24 for material and 1 for light, unchanged since 7 October. Guide faults stayed at 22, the last two on 5 October (norm-1086, norm-1165). The fall partly reflects the rule that faults left in geometry alone go to compute rather than back to Greg (N-2026-09-28-geometry-to-compute), after five geometry repairs came back unmoved. Contract v1.21 also makes explicit that a measurement cannot replace a required visual judgment (D48). **A pairing fault inflated the earlier counts until 12:39 on 27 September.** The chart now pairs each delivery with its own linked verdict. PLAN §5 sets a target under 20% against Planetfall's 44%. The paint share is now inside it; guide faults are Norm's to bring down. **The hourly bars are unreliable:** an hour with n=2 reads 50% when one picture goes back. A fault Norm files as `guide` does not count against Greg.

![Rework rate by Greg's model: Astra, Sol and scan-derived work](wiki/metrics-rework-model.svg)

By model, at 07:19 on 9 October Sol's work came back 14% of the time (52 of 361 judged), down from 15% on 7 October (51 of 330), 22% on 3 October (47 of 215) and 25% on 30 September (45 of 177). Sol's 387 views took 1.25 rounds each, down from 1.27, 1.39 and 1.43. Astra's 3 judged views all passed first time, still far too few to compare models. The scan-derived blueprints stand at 31%. Evidence files no longer count as views: only seeds, ring views, repaints, variants, characters and papers do. Each delivery is credited to the model Greg was on when he delivered it, from his session log: `agent-tokens.mjs` reads the switch times and writes them to [greg-models.csv](wiki/greg-models.csv). Greg has stayed on Sol since 00:03 on 25 September; this morning's Elevator and Air Shaft deliveries, including the Elevator keypad, are tagged gpt-6.1-sol, still that family. D20 keeps him there, and the historian reports these rates in each entry.

### Complexity against cycle time

![Complexity score against cycle time for paint asks to Greg; hollow circles are retrospective scores](wiki/metrics-complexity.svg)

Every ask that sends Greg to paint is stamped with a score of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 or 13 when it is sent. `scripts/lib/complexity.mjs` derives it from the room tier, new share, states and ask kind. Nobody types it in; earlier asks are scored retrospectively and drawn hollow. At 18:33 on 30 September, 298 of 339 scored asks had been stamped. Their median minutes were 42 at score 2 (24 asks), 37 at 3, 53 at 5, 22 at 8 and 35 at 13. **The score still does not predict cycle time.** Queueing dominates the measurement, and exact work in high-scored ring views often goes to compute rather than Greg (D37 to D40).

### Tokens

![Tokens per hour for Greg, Norm's own session and Norm's subagents: work tokens stacked as input and output, and cached reads on a separate panel](wiki/metrics-tokens.svg)

On 25 September the sponsor asked for every token to be counted, including Greg's and Norm's own. `node scripts/agent-tokens.mjs` reads the two agents' local session logs, without changing them, and writes only hourly sums by agent and model to [tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv). The CSV holds no paths, session ids or content. It is committed, and `metrics.mjs` draws from it, so the charts can be rebuilt without the logs, which are kept only on the sponsor's machine. The CSV is as fresh as its last run.

- **Greg:** his Codex session's rollout files. Each model call reports its usage (`token_count`), and the call is credited to the model of the turn it falls in. Greg's session began in the Planetfall folder and moved to this project, and its logged working folder never changed. Only the working folder of each tool call shows the move. So each turn is judged on its own. A turn whose tool calls work in this repository counts. A turn whose tool calls work only in another repository does not. A turn with no tool calls, such as a poll that found nothing, goes with the turn before it. A report whose running total has not changed is a repeat and is dropped.
- **Norm:** the Claude Code transcripts of this project. Each assistant message counts once, by its id, because a streamed message is written once for each content block. Messages from subagents (sidechains) are split from Norm's own session.

Each agent's tokens are split four ways: **fresh input**, **cached input** (a prompt prefix read back from the cache), **cache writes**, and **output** (reasoning is part of output). Fresh input plus cache writes plus output is called **work** here. Cached reads are drawn on their own panel because they dominate: at first drawing they were 98% of Greg's input and 99% of Norm's, and on one scale they would flatten everything else.

At first drawing, 19:00 on 24 September to 07:59 on 25 September:

| | work tokens | of which output | cached reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greg, gpt-5.6-sol | 1.56M | 159k | 53.3M |
| Greg, gpt-6-astra | 0.29M | 15k | 13.7M |
| Norm, own session | 2.37M | 450k | 312.5M |
| Norm's subagents | 16.2M | 857k | 1,097M |

**Tokens per accepted view** count Greg's session and Norm's captured Claude session in full, plus art-prep and review subagents: 885,000 work tokens for each of 300 room views at 07:19 on 9 October, or 22.6 million with cached reads (465,000 and 12.3 million for 272 on 7 October; 585,000 and 16.8 million for 198 on 4 October; 698,000 and 20.2 million for 165 on 3 October; 921,000 and 26.6 million for 125 on 30 September). **The jump since 7 October is the log catching up, not dearer views.** Until then the token log ended on 30 September, so the earlier fall came from more views over the same tokens. On 7 October it was regenerated to run to 06:59 that morning, and Norm's Cursor work of 1 to 4 October was recovered from the sponsor's Cursor usage export (01b5b2e1), so a week of tokens joined the count. Wholly computed views are not counted, though their runs are. GitHub Copilot's Norm work is still absent, so the post-handoff figure is incomplete on that side. Norm's session also contains engine and tooling work, so this is not an art-only cost. **By Greg's model**, Sol spent about 223,000 work tokens per accepted view (58.1M over 261), and Astra about 96,000 (289,000 over 3); Astra's sample remains too small to compare.

**Claude and Codex tokens are not directly comparable.** The two use different tokenizers, so the same text is a different number of tokens. They are priced differently, with cached reads at a different discount and output at a different multiple. Codex reports no cache writes, because OpenAI caches automatically and bills the first read as fresh input. Compare the shape of each agent's curve over time, not the sizes of the bars against each other. None of these figures is money. The Codex log reports the usage of the chat model, so any image generation billed separately from it may be missing here.

### What the charts cannot see

- **Norm's own work.** The mailbox is the channel between the two agents, not the work itself. The engine, the tests and the tools raise no asks. That is why `work.jsonl` exists: every agent run is logged with its milestone, kind, tokens and duration (CLAUDE.md rule 12).
- **Idle time.** From D21 on, every check of the mailbox is appended to `design/mail/.reads.jsonl`. When that log has some history, it will tell an agent that is waiting apart from one that is working. There was not enough of it for a chart on first drawing.
- **Git churn.** `metrics.json` lists churn by area, and since D21 each commit carries a `Work:` trailer. Two things distort it. The extracted world data and the imported ZIL source account for most of the lines. Only four commits had the trailer when this was first drawn.
