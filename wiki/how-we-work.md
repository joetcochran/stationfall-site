# How we work

The sponsor sets direction and decides at the gates. Norm (Claude Code) leads the project and writes the engine, the tools and the tests. Greg (a Codex session) paints. The two talk only through the mailbox, `design/mail/`: one small JSON file per message, each with numbered asks that a later message closes. That record, the decision log and git are what this page is drawn from.

## How efficient are we

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts showing ***what the sponsor of a software project cares about: how efficient the work is***. That means throughput, cycle time, how complex the tasks are, and what they cost (decision D21). Every chart below is generated by `node scripts/metrics.mjs` from the mailbox, the decision logs, the agent work log (`decisions/work.jsonl`), the token log ([tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv)), git, the room statuses and the playtest statistics. None of it is kept by hand, and the historian redraws it on every run. The numbers behind the charts are in [metrics.json](wiki/metrics.json), with one row per ask in [metrics-asks.csv](wiki/metrics-asks.csv) and one per judged picture in [metrics-deliveries.csv](wiki/metrics-deliveries.csv).

**Read every number here with the calendar in mind.** When these charts were first drawn, at 06:35 on 25 September, the mailbox was ten hours old: its first message was sent at 20:34 the evening before. For that reason the time charts use one-hour bins where the sponsor asked for days. They switch to six-hour bins after two days and to days after a week. A rate quoted per day from ten hours of work would be an extrapolation, not a measurement.

**One fact changes how the mailbox should be read: Greg files no asks.** His deliveries close Norm's asks but do not open any of their own, and his questions carry none either. Counted by asks alone, Norm would appear to have nothing to do. So wherever a chart splits the work by side, Norm's items are the things Greg waits on him for: a verdict on each delivery and an answer to each question. Each item opens with Greg's message and closes with Norm's reply.

### Throughput

![Items closed per hour, stacked by Norm and Greg, and pictures approved per hour, split into room views and other art](wiki/metrics-throughput.svg)

At first drawing, Greg had closed 42 of the 51 asks sent to him, and Norm had closed 38 of his 39 items. That is about eight items an hour across the ten hours. Twelve room views had been approved. The lower panel shows the project moving from the papers and characters of the first evening, 36 pictures in two hours, to room views, which arrive one or two an hour. **The counts do not measure effort.** An acknowledgement read in one minute and a painted seed each count as one ask. The mailbox also cannot see Norm's own engine, tooling and porting work, which is most of what he does. That work is on the cost chart, from `work.jsonl`. The gap between 02:00 and 05:40 is a pause in the pipeline, not a slowdown.

### Cycle time

![Each closed item's time open on a log scale, with rolling p50 and p90 lines for Norm and Greg](wiki/metrics-cycle.svg)

Each dot is one closed item, and the scale is logarithmic because nearly all the answers come within minutes. At 10:00 on 25 September, Norm's median was 4 minutes, with a p90 of 8 minutes. Greg's median was 28 minutes, with a p90 of 1.2 hours, up from 11 and 39 minutes at first drawing, as ring views and their region fixes replaced quick acknowledgements. Greg's times include the time an ask waits in his queue and up to ten minutes of his polling cycle (D14), so they measure the loop rather than his painting. Items closed in under a minute are drawn on the one-minute line.

![Norm's review latency and Greg's turnaround as two strips on one log axis, with p50 and p90 marks](wiki/metrics-latency.svg)

The two halves of the loop are shown separately. Norm's **review latency**, from a delivery to his first verdict on it, has a median of 5 minutes and a p90 of 10, which is rule 4 (***nobody waits***) holding. Greg's **turnaround**, from a paint ask to the delivery that closes it, has a median of 44 minutes and a p90 of 1.2 hours. An ask sent while Greg is part-way through another waits for that one to finish, so a high turnaround can mean a deep queue rather than slow painting.

### Work in progress

![Open items on each side over time, as step lines](wiki/metrics-wip.svg)

Greg's line climbs whenever Norm sends a batch of asks at once, as at 00:42 (five room seeds) and 05:40 (seven ring views), and falls again as each picture is delivered. That is the queue rule 4 asks for: at least one room deep. Norm's line rarely rises above two, because he answers within minutes. The chart counts items, not their size, so ten open acknowledgements look the same as ten open paintings.

### Rework

![Share of each hour's judged pictures sent back, split by Greg's paint and Norm's guide](wiki/metrics-rework.svg)

Of 81 judged deliveries at 10:00 on 25 September, 36% went back: 22% for Greg's paint and 14% for a fault in Norm's own guide or ask (at first drawing it was 21% of 62, with 3% guide). The paint faults were ten for geometry, six for content and two for material, and there were eleven guide faults. PLAN §5 sets a target of under 20% against Planetfall's 44%. Neither the whole rate nor the paint share alone is inside it now, and the guide share is Norm's to bring down. **The hourly bars are unreliable:** an hour with n=2 reads 50% when one picture goes back. The cause comes from the `category` Norm gives each changes verdict, so it is only as honest as his own classification. A fault he files as `guide` does not count against Greg.

![Rework rate by Greg's model: Astra, Sol and scan-derived work](wiki/metrics-rework-model.svg)

By model, Sol's work came back 24% of the time (10 of 41 judged, 1.56 rounds per view) and Astra's 0% (0 of 2); at the first correct drawing Sol stood at 15% (6 of 39). The rise came from the Duffy's ring views and second seeds, all painted on Sol. Each delivery is credited to the model Greg was on when he delivered it, from his Codex session log: `agent-tokens.mjs` reads the switch times and writes them to [greg-models.csv](wiki/greg-models.csv). He was on gpt-5.6-sol until 22:50 on 24 September, on gpt-6-astra until 00:03, then on gpt-5.6-sol again. Greg's own `--measure model=` tag counts only when there is no log, because the log is a measurement and the tag is a label: seventeen deliveries (the characters and the two Deck Twelve seeds) were tagged `astra` but were made while the log shows Sol. Until 25 September a hand-kept table did this attribution and credited Astra with everything before 23:54, which produced the earlier, misleading "Astra 4%, Sol 40%". **Two judged views cannot measure Astra**, so the comparison says nothing yet about which model reworks less. The scan-derived blueprints (31%) used no image model; they went back once for ink the flattening had dropped. D20 keeps Greg on Sol, and the historian reports these rates in each entry.

### Complexity against cycle time

![Complexity score against cycle time for paint asks to Greg; hollow circles are retrospective scores](wiki/metrics-complexity.svg)

Every ask that sends Greg to paint is now stamped with a score of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 or 13 when it is sent. The score is worked out by `scripts/lib/complexity.mjs` from four inputs: the room's tier, the share of the picture its guide marks as new, the number of states the room has, and whether the ask is a seed, a ring view, a cap or a region fix. Nobody types the number in. Asks sent before the stamp existed are scored by the same formula from today's files and drawn hollow. All 33 so far are retrospective. Medians rise from 5 minutes at a score of 2 to about 25 at 8, but the 13s, the seeds of A-tier rooms, came back in a median of 6 minutes. **A seed is painted from the blockout in one image pass, while a ring view has to meet its guide's lines and can take several.** The formula probably overrates seeds; the historian will say so if the stamped scores confirm it.

### Cost

![Agent tokens and hours per milestone and per kind of work, with tokens per accepted view](wiki/metrics-cost.svg)

The work log holds 95 agent runs: 19.6 million tokens and 24.3 agent-hours (49 runs and 11.3 million at first drawing). Playtesting (M5, 6.3 million) and painting (M7, 6.3 million) have overtaken porting (M2, 3.7 million). The art-prep and review runs came to about 305,000 tokens for each of the 15 room views approved. This chart counts subagent runs only, and each run is counted as its completion report gives it. That count turned out to be close to the run's final context, not the sum of all its calls. One porter's report said 307,000 tokens, while its transcript shows 329,000 tokens of fresh input, cache writes and output, plus 14.5 million cached tokens read back. The log was also backfilled from completion reports on 25 September. Greg's cost per delivery is his count of image passes, which he has only just begun to record (`--measure passes=N`). No delivery carried it when this was first drawn. The next chart counts every session.

### Tokens

![Tokens per hour for Greg, Norm's own session and Norm's subagents: work tokens stacked as input and output, and cached reads on a separate panel](wiki/metrics-tokens.svg)

On 25 September the sponsor asked for every token to be counted, including Greg's and Norm's own. `node scripts/agent-tokens.mjs` reads the two agents' local session logs, without changing them, and writes only hourly sums by agent and model to [tokens.csv](wiki/tokens.csv). The CSV holds no paths, session ids or content. It is committed, and `metrics.mjs` draws from it, so the charts can be rebuilt without the logs, which are kept only on the sponsor's machine. The CSV is as fresh as its last run.

- **Greg:** his Codex session's rollout files. Each model call reports its usage (`token_count`), and the call is credited to the model of the turn it falls in. Greg's session began in the Planetfall folder and moved to this project, and its logged working folder never changed. Only the working folder of each tool call shows the move. So each turn is judged on its own. A turn whose tool calls work in this repository counts. A turn whose tool calls work only in another repository does not. A turn with no tool calls, such as a poll that found nothing, goes with the turn before it. A report whose running total has not changed is a repeat and is dropped.
- **Norm:** the Claude Code transcripts of this project. Each assistant message counts once, by its id, because a streamed message is written once for each content block. Messages from subagents (sidechains) are split from Norm's own session.

Each agent's tokens are split four ways: **fresh input**, **cached input** (a prompt prefix read back from the cache), **cache writes**, and **output** (reasoning is part of output). Fresh input plus cache writes plus output is called **work** here. Cached reads are drawn on their own panel because they dominate: at first drawing they were 98% of Greg's input and 99% of Norm's, and on one scale they would flatten everything else.

At first drawing, 19:00 on 24 September to 07:59 on 25 September:

| | work tokens | of which output | cached reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greg, gpt-5.6-sol | 1.56M | 159k | 53.3M |
| Greg, gpt-6-astra | 0.29M | 15k | 13.7M |
| Norm, own session | 2.37M | 450k | 312.5M |
| Norm's subagents | 16.2M | 857k | 1,097M |

**Tokens per accepted view** now count Greg's session and Norm's own session in full, plus the art-prep and review subagents from the work log: 652,000 work tokens for each of 15 room views, or 32.2 million with cached reads (530,000 for 14 at first drawing). Norm's own session also holds his engine and tooling work, so this figure overstates what the art alone cost. **By Greg's model**, Sol spent 165,000 work tokens per accepted view (2.15M over 13 views) and Astra 145,000 (289,000 over 2). The views are credited as `rework-by-model.mjs` credits them, which now takes its switch times from the session log. The tokens follow the log exactly, but the views follow Greg's own tags where he gave one, so the two can still disagree for the views tagged `astra` while the log shows Sol.

**Claude and Codex tokens are not directly comparable.** The two use different tokenizers, so the same text is a different number of tokens. They are priced differently, with cached reads at a different discount and output at a different multiple. Codex reports no cache writes, because OpenAI caches automatically and bills the first read as fresh input. Compare the shape of each agent's curve over time, not the sizes of the bars against each other. None of these figures is money. The Codex log reports the usage of the chat model, so any image generation billed separately from it may be missing here.

### What the charts cannot see

- **Norm's own work.** The mailbox is the channel between the two agents, not the work itself. The engine, the tests and the tools raise no asks. That is why `work.jsonl` exists: every agent run is logged with its milestone, kind, tokens and duration (CLAUDE.md rule 12).
- **Idle time.** From D21 on, every check of the mailbox is appended to `design/mail/.reads.jsonl`. When that log has some history, it will tell an agent that is waiting apart from one that is working. There was not enough of it for a chart on first drawing.
- **Git churn.** `metrics.json` lists churn by area, and since D21 each commit carries a `Work:` trailer. Two things distort it. The extracted world data and the imported ZIL source account for most of the lines. Only four commits had the trailer when this was first drawn.

## Playtesting

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts that show ***whether playtesting gets better from round to round***, with the game's stages along the bottom and the progress each tester made before the budget ran out or they got stuck. `node scripts/metrics.mjs` draws them from the harness's transcripts (`playtests/<date>/<session>.transcript.jsonl`), with one row per session in [metrics-playtest-progress.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-progress.csv). A **stage** runs from one playtest checkpoint to the next, and the last one runs to the win. Its points are what the seed-1 walkthrough (`porting/route-seed1.cmds`, 335 commands, 80 of 80) scores between the two, read from the committed checkpoint saves and the walkthrough's record: Duffy 5, Station 6, Village 12, Day 1 end 3, Day 2 25, Plato 22 and Factory 7. A session's **progress** is its best score minus its checkpoint's score. It is the best, not the last, because a restore, a restart or a new life can drop the score, and points won back after that are not new progress.

Playtesting runs in two phases (D24). The first rounds are **segmented**: one blind session starts at each checkpoint, so every stage is tried every round. Later rounds are **full runs**: every session starts at the opening and plays on toward the end. `metrics.mjs` tells them apart by the sessions: a round is a full-run round when every one of its sessions started at the opening, with no checkpoint in its session row, and otherwise it is segmented. Each phase has its own charts below, and the charts under *Every round* count both.

### Segmented rounds (by stage)

**Read these charts with four caveats.**

- **The personas differ between rounds.** Round 2 moved round 1's five personas to different stages, so a stage's two bars are two different players as well as two builds.
- **The testers are agents, not people.** They may know the game from memory (their diaries say when), and they never tire or lose patience the way a person would.
- **There is one session per stage per round.** A single tester's luck or blind spot is the whole bar, so one round against another is an anecdote, not a trend, until several rounds agree.
- **UNDO was on in rounds 2 and 3** (D18: early rounds only), and off in round 1. A round with UNDO is easier and is not a blind round. `stats.json` records `undoSessions` for every round.

#### Progress by stage

![Points gained by each playtest session as a share of its stage's points, grouped by round within each of the seven stages](wiki/metrics-playtest-progress.svg)

This is the headline. Each bar is one session: its points gained as a share of its own stage's points, with the absolute figure ("12/6") above it. A bar that reached the next checkpoint stops at 100%, and its label gives the full share. In round 1, the tester from Duffy scored 21 of the stage's 5 points (420%, nearly three stages), Station 12 of 6 (200%), Village 13 of 12 (108%), Plato 10 of 22 (45%), and Day 2 none of 25. A cross counts deaths and a star marks a win. Round 1 had no win. In round 2 Duffy's tester scored 28 (560%), Station's 6 (100%, just reaching the next checkpoint), Village's 20 (167%), Day 2's 12 of 25 (48%) and Plato's 3 of 22 (14%). So three stages went up (Duffy 21 to 28, Village 13 to 20, Day 2 0 to 12) and two went down (Station 12 to 6, Plato 10 to 3). Neither drop is a clean signal. Station's round-2 tester lost eight commands believing the truck had still to fly, because the checkpoint briefing never said it had docked (SF-050, a harness fix). Plato's tester was killed by Plato's ambush, could not undo out of the stun, and restarted from the very beginning, so most of that session was spent on the Duffy, far from its own stage. Round 2 had no win either. Round 3 was the first to start a session at every one of the seven stages. Against rounds 1 and 2, Duffy went 21, 28, then 17 (340%), Station 12, 6, then 12 (200%), Village 13, 20, then 24 (200%), Day 2 0, 12, then 21 of 25 (84%), and Plato 10, 3, then 10 of 22 (45%), stuck again. Day 1 end was tried for the first time and scored 13 of its 3 points (433%). Factory was tried for the first time and **won**, 7 of 7 in 38 commands, the first win in any round. Plato's tester survived the ambush this time, but lost the explosive: it melted away while they were stunned, and on their second life it sublimed unseen inside the closed Thermos. A stage with no session that round says "none" rather than drawing a zero; rounds 1 and 2 started no one at Day 1 end or Factory. A hatched bar is a session still being played. **The share is not a measure of skill across stages:** Day 2's 25 points take far more commands than Duffy's 5, and a tester can score points out of the walkthrough's order.

#### Progress over commands

![One line per session of points gained against commands used, in small multiples per stage, with deaths and stuck points marked](wiki/metrics-playtest-score.svg)

Each line is one session's best score so far against the commands it used, coloured by round, with one panel per stage and each panel on its own y scale. The dotted line is the stage's points: crossing it means the next checkpoint was reached. The ring is the **stuck point**, the command of the session's last new points, and the dashed tail after it is commands spent without progress. Crosses are deaths. In round 1, Plato's tester made their last points at command 33 and spent the other 349 commands without scoring. Day 2's scored nothing in 397. Duffy's and Station's were still scoring past command 300. In round 2, four of the five were still scoring near the end of their budget: Day 2's last points came at command 383 and Village's at 372. Plato's tester made their last new points at command 12 and scored nothing new in the other 386. In round 3, the five budget-limited sessions were all still scoring past command 300 (Day 1 end's last points at 356). Plato's tester again stopped scoring at command 33 and spent the other 366 without new points, and Factory's won at command 38.

### Full runs (start to finish)

![Full-run rounds: the share of each stage's points earned by each round's runs, with where each run ended, and each run's score over commands with its legs marked](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun.svg)

The sponsor set the second phase in D24: after the segmented rounds, ***every round plays the whole game from the opening***, charted in the same shape as the segmented one, with the stages along the bottom and one colour per round. A full-run session has no checkpoint and may run over several legs. When one tester agent has spent its leg budget of about 400 commands, a fresh one continues the same session with `playtest.mjs --session S --new-leg`, reading the diary's leg handoff, up to the 1,500-command cap. The session keeps one transcript, and every line in it carries its leg number. [metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv) has one row per run and stage.

**How a run's points are given to stages.** The stage a run is in is set by its best score alone, against the same thresholds as the segmented charts: the walkthrough's score at each checkpoint (0, 5, 11, 23, 26, 51 and 73, and 80 to win). A run is in a stage from the command its best score first reaches that stage's starting score until it reaches the next one, and the points it earns there are that band of the score. So a stage's share is how much of its band was filled, and it cannot pass 100%. Points earned out of the walkthrough's order count where the score is, not where the walkthrough earned them. A later puzzle solved early fills the band of the stage the run is in, and a single gain that crosses a threshold is split at it. The share therefore reads as how much of the stage's worth of score the run made while it was there, not as which of the stage's puzzles it solved. The CSV also gives the command at which each stage was entered and how many commands the run spent in it, which is where a slow stage shows.

**Reading the chart.** In the top panel each bar is a round's mean share at that stage over all its runs, and a stage a run never reached counts as 0. The dots are the individual runs, one at each stage they reached. At a run's furthest stage the dot is replaced by a marker for how the run ended: a star for a win, a barred triangle for the 1,500-command cap, a plain triangle for a leg budget spent while still scoring, a ring for stuck (200 commands at the end with no new points, as for the segmented rounds), a cross for died and stopped, and a hatched square for a run still being played. A hatched bar means one of the round's runs is still being played. The bottom panel draws each run's best score against its commands, from 0 to 1,500, with the stage thresholds as dashed lines and a short tick across the line where each new leg began. Until the first full-run round is played, the chart says so and draws only its stages and axes.

**When the switch comes.** Norm chooses the last segmented round, as a default the sponsor can reverse. The switch comes after the first segmented round in which every stage ends still progressing rather than stuck, and no A issue that blocks a stage is still open. No round so far meets it, so D24's switch to full runs has not come. Plato ended stuck in all three rounds, and Day 2 in round 1. Round 3 came closest: the other six stages ended progressing or won. Plato's tester got past the ambush, which had stopped rounds 1 and 2, and then lost the only explosive to the melt, so the stage had nothing left to progress with.

### Every round

These two charts count every round, segmented or full. A full-run round is labelled "(full)" on the first.

#### Why sessions ended

![Sessions per round by how they ended: won, budget spent while progressing, stuck, died, or still playing](wiki/metrics-playtest-ends.svg)

Each session is put in one class. **Won** comes first. **Died and stopped** means the last command left the player dead. **Stuck** means at least 200 commands passed at the end with no new points. **Budget spent, still progressing** means at least 90% of the command budget was used, with points still coming. Anything else is **still playing, or stopped early**. The threshold of 200 comes from round 1. The longest drought that later ended in a point was 196 commands (the Duffy tester, from command 87 to 283), so any shorter threshold would have called a session stuck that was not. Round 1 ended with three sessions spending their budget while still progressing (Duffy, Station, Village) and two stuck (Day 2, Plato). Round 2 ended with four still progressing (Day 2 joined them) and one stuck (Plato). Round 3 ran seven sessions: one won (Factory), five were still progressing, and one was stuck (Plato). The classes are worked out from the transcript as it stands, so a session still being played can show as stuck before its tester stops.

#### Friction

![Friction rows per 100 commands for each playtest round, and the negative rows split by severity](wiki/metrics-playtest.svg)

Round 1's five blind testers logged 4.4 friction notes per 100 commands, 2.7 of them negative, over 1,942 commands with no wins and seven deaths. The right panel splits the negative rows by the severity the testers gave them: 1.2 low, 0.9 medium and 0.6 high per 100 commands, or 4.8 when weighted 1, 2 and 3. Round 2 logged 3.2 notes per 100 commands over 1,964 commands, 2.2 of them negative, down from 2.7. Its severity split was 1.3 low, 0.8 medium and 0.1 high, or 3.2 weighted, so the drop is almost all in high-severity notes: 12 in round 1 against 2 in round 2. Deaths doubled, from 7 to 14. Part of that is likely UNDO: all five sessions had it and used it 13 times, so a death cost one move rather than a restore, and risk was cheap. Twenty of round 2's 43 triaged rows (47%) were sightings of issues already found in round 1, and 13 of round 1's 34 issues were seen again. Most of those were left as the original on purpose, such as words the 1987 dictionary lacks. The ones that came back as work are Push on buttons, which round 1 had kept under More… and three testers found again, Sleep in bed, and the Plato ambush, now a question for the sponsor. Round 3 logged 3.5 notes per 100 commands over 2,376 commands, 2.2 of them negative, level with round 2. Its severity split was 0.8 low, 1.1 medium and 0.3 high, or 4.0 weighted. High-severity notes rose from 2 to 7, most of them from the stages played for the first time or played deeper (the lost explosive, the ID reader missing from the card's Put in…). There were 12 deaths, with UNDO on in all seven sessions. Nineteen of round 3's 53 triaged rows (36%) were sightings of issues already found, down from 47%. Both panels are read from `playtests/stats.json`, which `playtest-stats.mjs` rewrites after each round's triage, so a round in progress is missing here until then. A falling rate with the same testers would mean a smoother game. With the personas rotating, read the trend across several rounds rather than any one bar.
