# How we tested playability

Every round of playtesting, 1 to 17, and what it found. The testers are blind: agents that play the port through the playtest harness without ever seeing its source, each in a persona it is given, and report what got in their way. This page holds the method, the rounds, the charts and the friction. Two pages go with it:

- [The playtesters' leaderboard](playtest-leaderboard.md) ranks every full run from the opening (round 7 on): who scored highest, filed the most kinds of friction, got the most frustrated or was blocked the most, and how much of that seems to come from the persona.
- [Playtests, round by round](playtests.md) holds the records: each session's diary, friction rows and outcome, with its round's triage and stats.

On 8 October the sponsor ***found that the playtest material read less as how the team works than as how the game's playability was tested***, and asked for it on pages of its own, with the leaderboard.

## How a playtest works

- **The harness.** A tester plays only through `scripts/playtest.mjs`, in typed mode (it types commands) or click mode (it picks from the same menus a mouse player sees). The harness keeps the transcript, every command with the game's reply, in `playtests/<date>/<session>.transcript.jsonl`. Transcripts are not published: they are mostly the game's own text.
- **Friction rows.** The moment something gets in the way, or pleases, the tester files a row through the harness: its kind (`blocked`, `bug`, `parse`, `not-understood`, `confusing`, `tedious`, `other`, or `nice` for praise) and its severity (low, medium or high). The harness adds the command count and the room. The tester also keeps a diary, which ends with a handoff to the next leg.
- **Triage.** After a round, its rows are linked to the issue registry (`playtests/REGISTRY.jsonl`) and each issue is tiered: **A**, a faithful or interface-only fix, built; **B**, a deviation from the original, which goes to the sponsor; **C**, left as the original. The round's triage document records each issue with its evidence from the original game. `playtests/stats.json` counts the rows per round from the harness's records alone.
- **Personas.** Each session is given a persona, a sentence about who is playing ("a retired librarian who reads the manual first and maps every room on graph paper"). The personas change every round.
- **Hosts.** Rounds 1 to 12 ran as Claude Code agents (headless `claude -p` testers or subagents, with some of round 7's legs on Cursor). Round 13 ran in GitHub Copilot Chat (D63), and its two click runs were finished by headless Claude testers. Round 14 went back to headless Claude Opus 5.5 testers, with the model now stamped on every record (216701b3). Rounds 15a, 15b and 15c ran headless on Claude Sonnet 5.5 (D84), 15b and 15c with the harness's guard against blind volleys (D86, below), and 15c with the D89 guardrails as well. Since D91, full runs from the opening use Opus 5.5 and early or segmented runs use Sonnet 5.5; round 15d was the first round under that rule (D92), and rounds 16a and 16b followed it. The last round, round 17, is to run on OpenAI's Astra model through the Codex CLI, with three new personas, after which playtesting is done (D102, D103). [Playtests, round by round](playtests.md) says more, and *What the tester models showed*, at the end of this page, compares the models from the records.
- **Round names.** New personas get a new round number; the same personas played again get a letter (15a to 15d). The sponsor applied the rule to round 16 on 13 October: its first round is now **16a**, and the rerun with the same personas is 16b (296f8a9b).

## Two phases, and the charts

On 25 September the sponsor asked for charts that show ***whether playtesting gets better from round to round***, with the game's stages along the bottom and the progress each tester made before the budget ran out or they got stuck. `node scripts/metrics.mjs` draws them from the harness's transcripts (`playtests/<date>/<session>.transcript.jsonl`), with one row per session in [metrics-playtest-progress.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-progress.csv). A **stage** runs from one playtest checkpoint to the next, and the last one runs to the win. Its points are what the seed-1 walkthrough (`porting/route-seed1.cmds`, 335 commands, 80 of 80) scores between the two, read from the committed checkpoint saves and the walkthrough's record. The stages are whatever checkpoints are committed, so adding one re-cuts the bands: the four checkpoints of 20 and 21 October split the old Plato band of 22 points into Plato 10 and the charge 12, and the charts now read every Plato bar against 10. The per-round figures in the prose below were written against the bands of their own day, when Plato was 22, so they do not match today's charts there; the seven named stages of rounds 1 to 6 were Duffy 5, Station 6, Village 12, Day 1 end 3, Day 2 25, Plato 22 and Factory 7, and the total is 80 either way. A session's **progress** is its best score minus its checkpoint's score. It is the best, not the last, because a restore, a restart or a new life can drop the score, and points won back after that are not new progress.

Playtesting runs in two phases (D24). The first rounds are **segmented**: one blind session starts at each checkpoint, so every stage is tried every round. Later rounds are **full runs**: every session starts at the opening and plays on toward the end. `metrics.mjs` tells them apart by the sessions: a round is a full-run round when any of its sessions started at the opening, with no checkpoint in its session row, and otherwise it is segmented; and each session is drawn by its own kind. Each phase has its own charts below, and the charts under *Every round* count both. **Round 14 mixed the two.** It ran two full runs beside one run from the Plato checkpoint (D70). Until 9 October a round counted as full only when every session started at the opening, so round 14 was drawn as segmented and its full runs were missing from the full-run charts and [the leaderboard](playtest-leaderboard.md). The sponsor ***noticed the gap***, and since 5b10d176 the two full runs are on the full-run charts and the leaderboard, and the veteran's run from Plato is on the segmented ones. Rounds 15a and 15b (D74, D86) are all full runs again.

## Segmented rounds 1 to 6 (by stage)

**Read these charts with four caveats.**

- **The personas differ between rounds.** Round 2 moved round 1's five personas to different stages, so a stage's two bars are two different players as well as two builds.
- **The testers are agents, not people.** They may know the game from memory (their diaries say when), and they never tire or lose patience the way a person would.
- **There is one session per stage per round.** A single tester's luck or blind spot is the whole bar, so one round against another is an anecdote, not a trend, until several rounds agree.
- **UNDO was on in rounds 2 to 7** (D18: early rounds only), and off in round 1 and from round 8 on. A round with UNDO is easier and is not a blind round. `stats.json` records `undoSessions` for every round.

## Full runs, round 7 on

### From segmented rounds to full runs

**When the switch comes.** Norm chooses the last segmented round, as a default the sponsor can reverse. The switch comes after the first segmented round in which every stage ends still progressing rather than stuck, and no A issue that blocks a stage is still open. Rounds 1 to 5 did not meet it. Plato ended stuck in each of rounds 1 to 3, and Day 2 in round 1. Round 3 came closest: the other six stages ended progressing or won. Plato's tester got past the ambush, which had stopped rounds 1 and 2, and then lost the only explosive to the melt, so the stage had nothing left to progress with. Round 4 does not meet it either, for the same reason. Plato ended stuck for the fourth round running (3 of 22, no new points after command 15), and Day 2's explosive melted as well, so that stage would have stalled too with a longer budget. No stage-blocking A issue is open: the melt is the original's, not a port bug. Round 5 does not meet it either: Plato and Day 1 end ended stuck. Taken without the ambush, as the score-drop investigation recommends, the picture is the same. Plato's tester survived the ambush with Floyd's help in all three lives (once only after an UNDO), lost the explosive to the melt in the first, and then spent 370 commands looking for where it goes: the safe, drilled before the checkpoint, is never named in what the tester remembers. Believing the card scrambled by a bug (it is the route's real state and harmless after the zapgun, SF-116), the tester restarted the checkpoint and lost their save (SF-114). Day 1 end's tester completed the stage's own 3 points at once and then scored nothing in 384 commands: a log-reader death and a checkpoint restart at command 176 cost half the leg, and the restart also reset the clock, so the ambush never came. Village, Duffy and Day 2 were still scoring. No stage-blocking A issue is open: round 5's A items are our own wording and interface faults, which slow a tester but block no stage. So the switch waits on a round in which Plato's and Day 1 end's testers keep scoring.

**Round 6 meets the condition, so it is the last segmented round.** Every stage ended progressing or won. Plato won from its checkpoint, Factory won, and the other five were still scoring when their budgets ran out. The longest flat tail was Day 1 end's, 100 commands after its last points at command 300. No A issue that blocks a stage is open. Round 6's nine A items (SF-134 to SF-142) are the harness's memories, the wording of our own can't-be-won notice, an article before our new plural words, the word "humans", a harness menu fault and four click-menu gaps. None stops a stage: each gap has another path in the same menus or in typed words. The foil reaches the pyramid through the pyramid's own menu, the ostrich's menu has Give, and the disposal hole is not needed to win. The only other open A, SF-082, is a rule for testers, not the game. So from round 7 every round is a full run from the opening (D24). This is Norm's default, which the sponsor can reverse. One caveat stands: with one session per stage (Q10), a single round is thin evidence, and Plato's win came only on its tester's third life, after eight deaths: to the ambush, to the exercise machine, and three times to the launch.

**Why the memories unblocked Plato.** A segmented tester starts in the middle of the game without the memory the character would have of getting there. At 06-plato the seed-1 route had already drilled the Commander's safe before the checkpoint. Round 5's Plato tester found the explosive but spent 370 commands looking for where it goes, because nothing they had been told mentioned the hole. From round 6 the briefing adds what the character remembers (`scripts/lib/memories.mjs`). Some lines are mechanical, from the route: who was met, the hole the drill made, and where each thing was put down. The rest are a few hand-written lines per checkpoint. All are facts the character witnessed, never advice. Round 6's Plato tester read that they had drilled a pencil-sized hole in the safe, saw that the explosive was the size of a pencil, and knew where it went. This is a harness fix, not a game change. A player who starts from the opening did those things themselves, so the full runs need no memories. But the memories have to be exactly right. Round 6 found two wrong lines and one misleading one: 07-factory said the jammer had been turned on, when the route's last step had turned it off; its Floyd line had not caught up with him; and 04-day1-end's line read as if the bag, the headlamp and the nip all came from one ceiling panel. The audit of every line against the checkpoint states is SF-134.

Until 12 October rounds 11 on were one "late" chart; the sponsor asked that rounds 15 on get their own, and confirmed the boundaries Norm proposed: segmented rounds 1 to 6 on their own charts, then early 7 to 10, middle 11 to 14 and current 15 on.

The sponsor set the second phase in D24: after the segmented rounds, ***every round plays the whole game from the opening***. It was first charted like the segmented rounds, with the stages along the bottom. When round 7 began, the sponsor found that ***a stage axis makes no sense for a run from the opening and asked for commands, or turns, instead***, so the chart now plots score over commands and shows the stages as bands on the score axis. A full-run session has no checkpoint and may run over several legs. When one tester agent has spent its leg budget of about 400 commands, a fresh one continues the same session with `playtest.mjs --session S --new-leg`, reading the diary's leg handoff, up to the 1,500-command cap. The session keeps one transcript, and every line in it carries its leg number. [metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv](wiki/metrics-playtest-fullrun.csv) has one row per run and stage.

**How a run's points are given to stages.** The stage a run is in is set by its best score alone, against the same thresholds as the segmented charts: the walkthrough's score at each checkpoint (0, 5, 11, 23, 26, 51 and 73, and 80 to win). A run is in a stage from the command its best score first reaches that stage's starting score until it reaches the next one, and the points it earns there are that band of the score. So a stage's share is how much of its band was filled, and it cannot pass 100%. Points earned out of the walkthrough's order count where the score is, not where the walkthrough earned them. A later puzzle solved early fills the band of the stage the run is in, and a single gain that crosses a threshold is split at it. The share therefore reads as how much of the stage's worth of score the run made while it was there, not as which of the stage's puzzles it solved. The CSV also gives the command at which each stage was entered and how many commands the run spent in it, which is where a slow stage shows.

**Reading the chart.** Each line is one run: its best score so far (y, 0 to 80) against the commands it has used over all its legs (x, 0 to the 1,500-command cap). A line steps up where the run scored and runs flat where it did not, so a steep line is a run moving fast, and a long flat stretch is one searching. The stages are horizontal bands on the score axis, lightly alternating in shade and named on the right with their range: Duffy 0-5, Station 5-11, Village 11-23, Day 1 end 23-26, Day 2 26-51, Plato 51-73 and Factory 73-80. A line crossing into a band has reached that stage's starting score, and the width of a band's crossing shows how many commands the stage took. Lines are coloured by round, with the same round colours as the segmented charts, and each further run in a round is a lighter shade of it. The legend names every run (for example "r7 typed" or "r7 click A"), and the latest round's runs are also named at their line ends with their best score. The sponsor found ***rounds 7 to 12 on one chart too busy to read, and asked for an early and a late group, and a way to pick out a single round***. The runs were split at round 11, and since 12 October also at round 15 (`MIDDLE_FULL_ROUND` and `CURRENT_FULL_ROUND` in `metrics.mjs`), and the CSV's `group` column (early, middle or current) says which chart each run is on. Above each chart, the buttons pick one round ("r8", say): its runs are drawn at full strength and named at their line ends, and the other rounds are faded, not removed, so the scale stays. "All" restores the chart. The sponsor then ***asked for two more filters, by the kind of session and by how it ended, that combine with the round's***: the "Type" row picks the typed runs, the click runs (A and B together) or one click letter, and the "Outcome" row picks the runs with one outcome. A run is drawn at full strength only when it matches every row's pick, and a note under the rows says how many runs match. Every full round together is still drawn as `metrics-playtest-fullrun.svg`, which the journal's earlier entries show. A run still being played is dashed. A cross on a line is a death, and a short tick across it is where a new leg began. The small ring is the run's **stuck point**, its last new best, as on the segmented score chart. The marker at a line's end is the run's outcome, which `scripts/lib/playtest-outcome.mjs` derives from the transcript, the cap and the diary's last handoff, never by hand. A star is a win and a barred triangle is the 1,500-command cap reached without a win. A ring is **stuck**: the run stopped short of the cap because the tester judged the game unwinnable or was blocked, which the final handoff says, or the game's own "can no longer be won" notice came after the run's last new best. A cross is a run that stopped short on a death. An empty square is a run cut off short with no reason given and never continued, and a hatched square is a run of the latest round still being played. Hover a line for its session, type, legs, deaths and outcome. [The playtests page](playtests.md) shows the same outcome for every session, with its diary, friction rows and triage, and can be browsed by round, type and session. Until the first full-run round is played, the chart says so and draws only its axes and stage bands. The stage-by-stage shares described above are no longer drawn, but they stay in the CSV and in `metrics.json`.

### Why sessions ended

![Sessions per round by how they ended: won, budget spent while progressing, stuck, died, or still playing](wiki/metrics-playtest-ends.svg)

Each session is put in one class. **Won** comes first. **Died and stopped** means the last command left the player dead. **Stuck** means at least 200 commands passed at the end with no new points. **Budget spent, still progressing** means at least 90% of the command budget was used, with points still coming. Anything else is **still playing, or stopped early**. The threshold of 200 comes from round 1. The longest drought that later ended in a point was 196 commands (the Duffy tester, from command 87 to 283), so any shorter threshold would have called a session stuck that was not. **Feedback volume and depth.** The second half of the sponsor's impression holds. Sonnet filed about a quarter as many rows per command as Opus (0.51 against 1.93), and wrote about 60% as many diary words. Its rows were not shorter (25 words of reason against 27), but fewer of its fault rows named a cause (15% against 21%) or proposed a fix (12% against 17%). Those two shares come from fixed word patterns ("because", "probably", "should", "would help" and the like), so they are rough. Triage linked every fault row of both models to an issue, so neither model filed rows that went nowhere; Sonnet simply filed fewer.

**Sycophancy.** Opus praises more: 39% of its rows against 24%, and 1.6 praise rows per death against 0.2. The records do not show praise rising when a run goes badly. Each run was cut into 100-command windows, and a window counts as bad when it had a death or no new best. Opus filed 0.84 praise rows per 100 commands in good windows and 0.68 in bad ones; Sonnet filed 0.21 and 0.09. Fault rows rose in the bad windows for both (Opus from 0.98 to 1.30, Sonnet from 0.17 to 0.47). Softening words in fault reports ("minor", "fair enough", "a bit") appear in 6% of each model's fault rows. Opus is the more generous reporter, but neither model's praise covers for a bad stretch.

**Reasoning and clues.** Opus's diaries state more hypotheses and plans per command (2.3 against 1.7) and revisit a theory more than twice as often (0.5 against 0.2 per 100 commands). Per diary word, though, Sonnet states hypotheses at much the same rate (4.5 per 1,000 words against 3.7): it writes less, rather than reasoning differently in what it writes. The spacetruck's course comes from the time:course table on Form QX-17-T: Opus's median run typed one course before its first docking, at command 31, and Sonnet's typed two, at command 51 (15a's typed three, at 69).
